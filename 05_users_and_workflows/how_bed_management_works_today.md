# How bed management works today

*Written up by Wardline's product researcher after two days shadowing the site team at Kingsmere (15 – 16 September 2026) and calls with Kisima and Sukamaju. `decision_lead_times.csv` is the companion table.*

## Kingsmere Royal Infirmary (UK)

### The day

| Time | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| 07:30 | Night site manager hands over to the day site manager | Clinical site managers |
| 08:00 | Bed state taken: beds open, beds occupied, patients in ED waiting for a bed | Site office |
| **08:30** | **Bed meeting.** Each division reports empty beds and "definite" and "possible" discharges for today. ED reports who is waiting. The alert level (OPEL) is set | Site manager chairs; divisional managers, matrons, ED nurse in charge, discharge team lead |
| 09:00 – 10:30 | Board rounds on each ward: every patient reviewed; discharges confirmed | Ward doctors, nurses, therapists |
| 09:15 | Staffing huddle: gaps for today and tonight; who can be moved | Matrons, e-rostering coordinator |
| 12:00 | Bed meeting. Are the morning's discharges happening? Extra discharge rounds requested if not | As 08:30 |
| **15:30** | **Bed meeting. Decisions for tonight and tomorrow:** open or close escalation beds; cancel tomorrow's planned operations | As 08:30, plus the Deputy Chief Operating Officer on bad days |
| 19:30 | Day hands over to night | Clinical site managers |

Weekly: Wednesday long-stay review (every patient in hospital 21 days or more); Thursday weekend planning meeting; theatre scheduling meeting (lists drafted six weeks ahead, confirmed at four, locked at two).

### The bed meeting sheet

One side of A4, produced by the site office from the EPR bed board and phone calls. For today it shows:

> beds empty now + discharges expected today − patients waiting in ED − expected emergency admissions − planned admissions = **predicted position at midnight**

"Expected emergency admissions" is the average for that weekday over the last six weeks. "Discharges expected" is what the wards say. The site team's working rule: *"Wards are optimistic. Knock a third off the possibles."*

There is nothing on the sheet about the day after tomorrow.

### What people do with a bad number

In rough order of how early they are used:

1. Chase discharges: phone calls, extra ward rounds, the discharge lounge opened longer.
2. Move patients who can move: "outlie" medical patients into surgical beds.
3. Open escalation beds on Ward 20, six at a time, and find nurses for them.
4. Cancel tomorrow's planned operations.
5. Care for patients in corridors ("temporary escalation spaces").
6. Ask neighbouring hospitals for help.

Steps 3 to 6 are usually decided the same day or the day before.

### Who is not in the room

- The local authority and the community trust, who control care packages, rehabilitation beds and care home places.
- Ward nurses and healthcare assistants.
- Night staff, by definition.
- Patients and families.

### Tools in use

The EPR bed board (real time), the bed meeting sheet (paper), a Power BI dashboard maintained by the information team (yesterday's data), ward whiteboards, a Teams chat, and the telephone. Several trusts in the region also use tools from the nationally funded data platform. One ward manager counted nine separate dashboards she is expected to look at.

## Kisima County Referral Hospital (Kenya)

- **Daily.** Each ward's nurse in charge writes the midnight count in the bed-state book. At about 07:30 the nursing officers give a verbal report to the Nursing Services Manager. The Medical Superintendent hears about problems by phone or WhatsApp.
- **There is no bed manager.** A ward that is full keeps admitting. The nurse in charge decides who shares.
- **Weekly.** Monday management meeting: staffing, supplies, money.
- **Monthly.** Ward tallies go into the national information system by the 15th.
- **Decisions with long lead times:** hiring locum nurses needs county approval (three to six weeks); oxygen cylinders, blood and antimalarial drugs are ordered weeks ahead and often arrive late.
- **Decisions a seven-day forecast might inform:** calling in off-duty nurses; borrowing oxygen cylinders from a neighbouring county; opening the corridor of the paediatric ward; asking the county for emergency funds.

## RSUD Sukamaju (Indonesia)

- **Daily.** The admissions desk (TPPRI) allocates a room to each patient, by ward, class and sex. The morning report goes to the directors by WhatsApp at about 07:30: beds available by ward and class, patients waiting in the emergency department.
- **When no bed of the right class is free,** the desk places the patient in another class ("titip") or the patient waits in the emergency department. If the wait is long the patient may be referred to another hospital.
- **A public display** shows bed availability to the insurer and the public. It is updated by hand and is often out of date.
- **Decisions a seven-day forecast might inform:** adding temporary beds (*tempat tidur tambahan*) in the paediatric ward during dengue season; rescheduling planned surgery; staff leave around Lebaran; asking the regency for emergency supplies.

## Three things the researcher wrote on the last page

1. "Everyone can tell you tomorrow. Nobody can tell you Thursday. But almost every decision I watched was about today or tomorrow."
2. "The number they argue about is not occupancy. It's *how many beds short are we*."
3. "At all three hospitals the people who would be most affected by a forecast being acted on — ward nurses, patients waiting for care at home — were the people I spoke to least."
