# Interview notes — Kingsmere Royal Infirmary (UK)

*Nine conversations, September – October 2026. Notes by Wardline's product researcher. Words in quotation marks are verbatim; the rest is summary. Participants agreed to be quoted by role.*

---

## K1. Clinical site manager (days), 14 years in role — 15 September

- Runs the 08:30 bed meeting. "I can tell you tomorrow morning within ten beds. I've been doing it since before you had a company."
- Cannot tell Thursday from Monday. "But be honest with me. If you told me on Monday that Thursday is bad, what would I do on Monday? I can't staff Ward 20 on a maybe."
- Thinks days three to five could matter for two things: booking bank nurses before the agencies get them, and telling surgeons on Tuesday rather than on the morning.
- "Don't show me occupancy. We're never over a hundred per cent on paper. Show me how many I'm short."
- Wants to tell the tool things it cannot know: a ward closing for norovirus, a care home that has stopped taking people, a strike.
- Has seen three forecasting tools come and go. "They were all very confident."

## K2. Ward manager, care of the elderly, 11 years in role — 15 September

- 28 beds. Most of her patients are over 80.
- "When the hospital's full, two things happen to my ward. They take my nurses for the escalation ward, and they lean on me for discharges. Your screen will make both happen earlier and more often."
- Last winter a patient of hers went home at eleven at night to an empty house. "She was back in two days. Nobody counts that."
- Counted the dashboards she is asked to look at: nine. "I look at two."
- Has not been asked about the product before today. Her healthcare assistants have not been asked at all.
- Would value one thing: "Tell me on Monday that Wednesday's staffing is going to be raided, so I can plan the skill mix."

## K3. Discharge coordinator, 9 years in role — 16 September

- "It counts beds. I work in people and packages of care."
- Says 14 of her 31 delayed patients that morning were waiting for something the council or a care home had to do. None of that is in the hospital's data.
- Describes the Friday push: "Everyone wants the numbers down for the weekend. Some of those come back on Monday. We call them failed discharges. The hospital calls them readmissions, if it calls them anything."
- Patients from the poorer estates wait longer for care at home: fewer family members who can take time off, fewer care agencies willing to travel there.
- Would use a forecast of *discharge delays by pathway* every day. Has no use for a forecast of occupancy.

## K4. ED nurse in charge, 7 years in role — 16 September

- "On the worst days I have twenty-odd patients who've been told they're being admitted and there's no bed. They're on trolleys in a corridor. Older ones mostly: they're the ones who wait longest."
- "Your screen was green on a day I had eleven in the corridor. So green means nothing."
- Wants one number: patients needing a bed by 08:00, 14:00 and 20:00.
- Worries that a forecast showing "manageable" would be used to refuse her requests for help.

## K5. Deputy Chief Operating Officer (our sponsor), 4 years in role — 22 September

- "This is the conversation I want to have with the executive team on a Monday. Give me the range. I can handle a range."
- Two things he wants to stop: cancelling operations on the day, and opening escalation beds at four in the afternoon with nobody to staff them.
- Believes last winter's short-notice escalation cost "well over a million". Has not seen the figures broken down.
- No budget line. A pilot under £75,000 can go through on a single quote; above that it is a procurement. Would fund it from winter money, which must be spent by 31 March.
- Needs something to show the trust board in January. "If it only works in shadow until April I've missed the winter and I've lost my argument."
- Asked what he would do if the forecast was badly wrong and a decision had been made on it: "It would be my decision, not the tool's. But I'd want to know you'd told me how wrong it can be."

## K6. Information analyst, 5 years in role — 22 September

- Built the hospital's Power BI flow dashboard. Runs a simple forecast of admissions: the same weekday averaged over the last six weeks.
- "Compare yourselves to that, and to 'same as today', on every page. If you don't, I will."
- "The accuracy page is the best thing in your product and you've hidden it three clicks down."
- Every ward reconfiguration breaks the mapping. "Ward 15 changed specialty last September. Did your model notice?"
- Concerned about the nightly file: "By the afternoon meeting it's fifteen hours old."
- Not opposed. "If it's better than mine I'll switch mine off. Show me."

## K7. E-rostering coordinator, 6 years in role — 23 September

- Rosters are published six weeks ahead. A seven-day forecast cannot change a roster.
- It could change *when* temporary shifts are requested. "A shift requested a week out fills about nine times in ten, mostly with bank. The same shift requested the night before goes unfilled about four times in ten, and when it does fill it's far more likely to be off-framework agency."
- Ward managers will not request shifts for beds that are not open yet. "If they book six nurses for an escalation ward that never opens, that's their budget and their name on it."
- Would look at days four to seven. "Nobody else will."

## K8. Consultant geriatrician, 16 years in role — 29 September

- Supportive of earlier warning. "Cancelling a hip replacement on the morning is cruel. If you can turn that into a phone call three days before, do it."
- Deeply uneasy about discharge. "A number on a screen becomes a target. If the forecast says we need forty discharges, forty will be found. The frailest are the easiest to move and the most likely to bounce back."
- "Whose judgement wins when your model and my ward round disagree? Write that down before you go live, not after."
- Asks whether anyone has looked at who gets cancelled and who gets rebooked quickly. "It won't be random."

## K9. Staff-side chair (joint union committee), 12 years as a nurse — 6 October

- Heard about the trial from a member, not from management.
- Three concerns: (1) redeployment decided by an algorithm; (2) forecasts used to justify lower staffing "because the computer says it'll be quiet"; (3) usage logs showing who looked at the screen and when.
- "Night staff and healthcare assistants weren't in your survey in any numbers. They're the ones who get moved."
- Not asking for it to stop. Asking for a seat at the table, an agreed statement of what the tool will never be used for, and a way for staff to report when it is wrong.
